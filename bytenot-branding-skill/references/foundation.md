## Voice

The PDF has no tone-of-voice chapter. This is derived from its values and its own prose, so it is
an interpretation; the test for any sentence is whether it would sit comfortably on page 5.

- **Plain.** Write for an owner who is not an engineer. Name the thing; skip the jargon, and if a
  technical word is needed, say what it does for them.
- **Honest.** Say what it costs, how long it takes, and what is not included. No superlatives, no
  "world-class", "cutting-edge", "seamless" or "revolutionary".
- **Calm.** Short declarative sentences. No urgency, no exclamation marks, no fear selling. The
  promise is the absence of worry, so the copy must not create any.
- **Owning.** "We", active verbs, responsibility taken: "we stay on", "let us handle that".
- **British spelling**, as the guide writes: colour, customisation, recognise.
- **Sentence case** for every heading and button; no all caps.
- Characteristic turns: "Worry not", "built like it's ours", "the team behind it".

## Reading work as a strategist and a marketer

Three questions to ask of anything before it ships, beyond whether it follows the rules:

- **Brand strategist: is it Bytenot's, or anyone's?** Would a stranger know who made it with the
  logo covered? The answer comes from the signature Orange, the Maroon, the symbol's soft shapes and the
  voice. Does it prove a value (ownership, craft, clarity, calm) rather than state it?
- **Head of marketing: does it move someone?** One audience (a business owner who cannot read
  code), one message, one next step. Is the promise, no worry, felt in the first screen? Is the
  proof real: live work, real people, real numbers?
- **Creative director: is it made well?** Hierarchy clear at a glance, one idea per surface,
  type and spacing exact, nothing decorative that the system does not already own.
